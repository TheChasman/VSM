# Electoral Reform Society (ERS) — Voting System Descriptions and Scores

Source: <https://electoral-reform.org.uk/voting-systems/types-of-voting-system/>
Retrieved: 11 November 2026

Descriptions below are summarised in my own words from the ERS pages, not copied verbatim. Scores were read directly from the page markup. Each score is out of 5 and shown on the site as stars.

The ERS rates three criteria and says the stars are only a rough guide:

- **Proportionality**: how closely seats in parliament reflect votes cast
- **Voter choice**: how far voters can choose between individual candidates
- **Local representation**: the connection between representatives and their communities

## System descriptions

### 1. First Past the Post (FPTP): P2 · C2 · L3
- **Mechanics:** Each constituency elects one member. Voters mark one X, and the candidate with the most votes wins, with or without a majority.
- **Where used:** Westminster, the US, Canada, India, and many former British colonies. Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, Malta, South Africa and Cyprus have all dropped it.
- **Points the ERS makes:** The results are disproportionate. The ERS's example is Labour winning about 63% of seats in 2024 on about 34% of the vote. It also notes:
  - Parties with geographically concentrated support gain an advantage.
  - Campaigning focuses on swing seats while safe seats are neglected.
  - Winners can have weak local mandates (Belfast South in 2015 was won on about 25%).
  - Voters face pressure to vote tactically.
  - The party with the most votes can still win fewer seats ("wrong winner" results: UK 1951 and Feb 1974, NZ 1978 and 1981).
- **Only point in its favour:** It usually produces single-party government.

### 2. Single Transferable Vote (STV): P4 · C5 · L5
- **Mechanics:** Each area elects several members. Voters rank candidates 1, 2, 3 and so on, and may rank as many or as few as they like. A candidate who reaches the quota is elected, and their surplus votes transfer to the voters' next preferences. When no one reaches the quota, the lowest candidate is eliminated and their votes transfer. This repeats until all seats are filled.
- **Where used:** Republic of Ireland, Northern Ireland, Malta, Scottish local elections, and parts of Australia (where it is called Hare-Clark). In the US it is called multi-member ranked-choice voting.
- **Points the ERS makes:**
  - Voters can choose between candidates within a party as well as between parties.
  - A vote for an independent is not wasted.
  - Constituencies can follow recognisable areas such as a town or county.
  - Most voters end up with a representative they backed.
  - The detail page lists no disadvantages. The ERS campaigns for STV.

### 3. Additional Member System (AMS / MMP): P4 · C3 · L3
- **Mechanics:** Voters have two ballots. The first elects a constituency member by FPTP. The second is a party vote, which is used to allocate list "top-up" seats so the overall result tracks party vote share. For example, a party that wins 5 constituency seats but is entitled to 8 receives 3 list seats. Scotland has 73 constituency and 56 regional list members. New Zealand allocates its top-up seats nationally.
- **Where used:** Scottish Parliament, London Assembly, Germany and New Zealand. In Germany and New Zealand it is called Mixed Member Proportional (MMP).
- **Pros (ERS):**
  - It is a compromise between FPTP and proportional representation (PR).
  - It is more proportional than Westminster.
  - Every party can win seats in every region.
  - Voters get a second representative to turn to.
- **Cons (ERS):**
  - Safe seats remain.
  - Parties control the lists.
  - It creates two classes of member, constituency and list.
  - The more constituency seats there are relative to list seats, the less proportional the result.

### 4. Alternative Vote Plus (AV+): P3 · C4 · L4
- **Mechanics:** Voters have two ballots. Constituency members are elected by AV, with ranked preferences and eliminations until a candidate passes 50%. The second ballot is for a party or one of its candidates, and it allocates a small number of county-level top-up seats. Where a party wins a top-up seat, its list candidate with the most personal votes takes it.
- **Where used:** Nowhere. The Jenkins Commission recommended it in 1998.
- **Points the ERS makes:** It is not fully proportional because about 80–85% of seats are constituency seats, which leaves too few top-up seats. A typical county would have six constituency members and one top-up member.

### 5. Two-Round System (TRS): P2 · C3 · L3
- **Mechanics:** A candidate with more than 50% in the first round is elected. Otherwise a second round is held, usually 2–3 weeks later. In most countries only the top two go through. For the French National Assembly, any candidate with at least 12.5% of registered voters goes through, or the top two if fewer qualify.
- **Where used:** France (presidential, legislative and regional elections) and presidential elections in at least 40 countries.
- **Pros (ERS):**
  - It is simple to understand and to count.
  - There is less need for tactical voting in the first round.
  - Constituency results are slightly more representative than under FPTP.
- **Cons (ERS):**
  - It is highly disproportionate and favours large parties.
  - Votes are still wasted.
  - Parties strike deals between rounds, and candidates may withdraw tactically.
  - The process takes weeks.
  - First-round tactical voting still happens.
  - Results can be surprising, as in France in 2002.
  - Smaller parties are squeezed out.

### 6. Alternative Vote (AV): P2 · C3 · L4
- **Mechanics:** Each constituency elects one member. Voters rank candidates. If no one has more than 50% of first preferences, the last-placed candidate is eliminated and their votes transfer, until someone has a majority.
- **Where used:** Australian House of Representatives, several Australian state parliaments, the Irish President, and many US states and cities (as Ranked Choice Voting).
- **Pros (ERS):**
  - Voters can back their favourite without wasting their vote.
  - There is less tactical voting.
  - Candidates are encouraged to campaign less divisively.
  - Fringe candidates tend to be eliminated early.
- **Cons (ERS):** AV is not PR. It can be *less* proportional than FPTP, and the ERS cites the 2015 general election as an example.

### 7. Supplementary Vote (SV): P2 · C3 · L3
- **Mechanics:** Voters mark a first choice and, if they wish, a second choice. If no one passes 50%, only the top two go to a run-off. Second choices for those two are then added. Ballots whose second choice is not in the top two are not counted in the run-off.
- **Where used:** It was the traditional system for UK mayors and Police and Crime Commissioners. The Conservative government replaced it with FPTP, and Labour is restoring it.
- **Pros (ERS):**
  - Winners need broader support than under FPTP.
  - Candidates have to campaign for second preferences.
  - The ballot uses X marks, which suits voters who use X-based ballots in other elections.
- **Cons (drawn from the ERS explanation):**
  - Ballots are exhausted when neither choice reaches the run-off.
  - Choosing a useful second preference gets harder as the number of candidates grows.
  - Voters can express fewer preferences than under AV.
  - It is not PR.

### 8. Party List PR (List PR): P5 · C3 · L2
- **Mechanics:** Each area elects several members. In the ERS's example, 65 areas would each elect 10 MPs instead of 650 single-member seats. Seats are allocated in proportion to party votes and filled from candidate lists. There are three main variants:
  - **Closed list:** the party's order decides who is elected.
  - **Open list:** voters' personal votes order the candidates.
  - **Semi-open list:** a hybrid of the two.
  Seats are allocated by the D'Hondt method, which slightly favours large parties, or the Sainte-Laguë method, which is more neutral.
- **Where used:** More than 80 countries, including Israel, the Netherlands, South Africa, Finland and Spain. In Israel and the Netherlands the whole country is a single constituency.
- **Pros (ERS):**
  - It is highly proportional.
  - New parties face lower barriers to entry.
- **Cons (ERS):**
  - Many parties win seats, and coalition government is normal.
  - The constituency link is weaker.
  - Smaller areas reduce proportionality.
  - Independent candidates are disadvantaged.
